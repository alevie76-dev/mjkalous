**Is It Love or Is It Respect?**

What happens when we confuse love with respect? It's a common mistake, and one that may be damaging our relationships.

Recently, I was speaking with a client who shared that she and her husband had enjoyed a meaningful conversation the previous evening. Their relationship had been struggling in the aftermath of a traumatic experience, resulting in confusion, miscommunication, and difficulty understanding one another.

During their conversation, her husband suddenly stopped and said, “Your respect gives me purpose.”

His words caused her to pause and reflect. She had always believed that respect must be earned, although she had often heard that everyone deserves respect. Yet she was struggling to respect her husband because of difficulties in how he processed information and responded to her needs.

Her experience raises an important question:

**Can I genuinely love someone whom I do not presently respect?** 

I believe we often confuse love with respect, and that misunderstanding can complicate communication and relational healing.

I offered her a simple thought:

“**Love is given. Respect is earned**.”

But that statement requires some clarification.

Every person deserves to be treated with basic human dignity, regardless of their behavior, accomplishments, or personal qualities. That is not the kind of respect I'm referring to here.

I'm speaking of the respect that grows from admiration, appreciation, and trust. Our regard for someone's character, judgment, integrity, and reliability develops as we observe their choices and conduct.

Everyone deserves dignity. But admiration, confidence, and trust are not automatically bestowed simply because someone occupies a particular role in our lives.

Love, on the other hand, is something different.

I believe every person is born with an innate desire to be **known, heard, and accepted.**

Put simply:

**“I see you. I hear you. I welcome you.”**

These three expressions are foundational to love, although they may sometimes resemble respect.

As a Christian, I believe I am called to love others, even those I do not know. That means taking a genuine interest in who they are—what they think and feel, what matters to them, what they enjoy, and what they hope to become.

It means listening to their beliefs, dreams, struggles, and stories. It means trying to understand their perspectives and the experiences through which they have come to see themselves, others, and the world.

And it means accepting them as people of inherent worth and dignity, even when I disagree with their beliefs or choices.

It is not my responsibility to change them, nor do I possess the power to save them.

My calling is to love them.

If I love someone, I will seek to treat them with honesty, kindness, and consideration. I will not steal from them, lie to them or about them, or covet what belongs to them.

I will want to encourage them, support them, and celebrate their victories. When they experience loss, I will mourn with them and seek to offer comfort.

**That is love.**

Respect, as I'm defining it here, develops when I recognize qualities worthy of admiration: integrity, responsibility, trustworthiness, sacrifice, compassion, and a heart for service.

And sometimes, love requires boundaries.

There may be circumstances in which maintaining a relationship becomes harmful or unhealthy. Sometimes loving someone means establishing limits, maintaining appropriate distance, or accepting that our presence is no longer welcome.

Please hear this: **Accepting someone does not mean agreeing with their beliefs, approving of their behavior, or surrendering our own convictions.**

We can love someone while holding them accountable for their actions.

We can recognize their worth without honoring their choices.

And we can wish them well without remaining in a relationship that continually causes harm.

Perhaps this is where our confusion between love and respect becomes most damaging. We sometimes withhold love because admiration or trust has been lost. Yet losing respect for someone's behavior does not necessarily mean we must stop loving the person.

I return, then, to my original thought:

**“Love is given. Respect is earned.”**

I don't claim to have all the answers, nor do I wish to damage relationships through futile arguments. I am, however, willing to listen, consider other perspectives, and engage in meaningful conversation—even when we disagree.

That, too, is an expression of love.

So, I leave you with a question:

**Can we continue to love someone whose choices we do not respect? And might that love create an opportunity for respect to grow again?**

May we all be blessed with both love and respect.
